What you found

A useful shortcut sent more cars to both bridges.

The shortcut itself stayed quick. The surprise came from its full path: every gold car used Riverside Road, the shortcut, and Millbrook Road. Both narrow bridges became busier.

This does not mean that new roads are bad. With fewer cars, the same shortcut saved about 8 seconds.

How the simulation works and where the numbers come from

This is a made-up map. In scientific terms it is a synthetic network: it shows one possible traffic pattern and does not represent Canberra or any real place.

The computer follows every car. A car speeds up when there is room and slows behind the car ahead. The technical rule is the Intelligent Driver Model, advanced at a fixed 0.25-second timestep. Nothing secretly adds a delay when the shortcut opens.

How does a car choose? The computer keeps one remembered time for each way. A finished trip nudges that way's remembered time toward what just happened. A quicker-looking way gets better odds, but it is never guaranteed. In technical terms, this is shared learning with one running estimate and a seeded, probabilistic choice.

What does “Let the next cars choose” do? It only lets the same simulated morning continue, then pauses again so you can look. The roads, traffic rate, choice rule, and saved random-number list do not change. Animation time is compressed, but the car rules are not.

Where do the answer numbers come from? They were made beforehand by two complete replays. Both reuse the same generated departure schedule and random seed: the same car start times and the same saved chance numbers. The only treatment difference is whether the shortcut is available. Scientists call this a paired counterfactual. The moving example starts with traffic already running (a warm-start illustration); its changing choice counts are not used as the scientific result.

The quiet test uses about 300 cars an hour. That is an average rate, not an exact count. Cars get irregular gaps between their start times, so a shorter period can land above or below that average. In this 20-minute scoring part, 96 cars started. Their average trip time was 5:19 with the shortcut closed and 5:11 with it open: about 8 seconds saved. 41 ÷ 96 ≈ 43% chose the shortcut.

The first 5:05 and 4:34 numbers are map estimates. They add road lengths and speed limits to make the computer's starting guess: 305 seconds for an old way and 274 for the shortcut. They are not timed trips. Moving cars accelerate and have slightly different speeds, so measured trip times differ.

The busy test uses about 860 cars an hour. Its 20-minute scoring part contains 280 car starts. The average changed from 5:31 to 5:44, or 3.8% longer. With the shortcut open, 106 of 280 trips used the shortcut: 106 ÷ 280 ≈ 38%. “Average” means all trip times were added, then divided by the number of trips. Clock times are rounded to the nearest second. We also let the simulation run longer and got a similar answer (the longer-horizon check).

There is no 4% score you must reach. A fair test and the direction of the change matter more than a big number. We tried 10 saved traffic lists, called seeds. 8 settled down enough to count; their average increase was 4.2%. Two were still changing, so we left them out.

Source. M. Treiber, A. Hennecke & D. Helbing, “Congested traffic states in empirical observations and microscopic simulations” , Physical Review E 62 (2000).
